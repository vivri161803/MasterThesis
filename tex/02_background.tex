\section{Foundations of Machine Learning and Large Language Models}
\label{sec:ml-foundations}

\subsection{From classical statistical models to neural language models}
\label{sec:classical-to-neural}

A language model assigns a probability to a sequence of tokens, or equivalently predicts the next token given its predecessors. Early language models estimated this distribution with $n$-gram counts over a training corpus, a formulation that traces back to Shannon's information-theoretic treatment of language as a stochastic process \citep{shannon1948mathematical}. $n$-gram models are simple and exact but suffer from data sparsity: most sequences of length $n$ never occur in any finite corpus, forcing smoothing heuristics and capping the context that can be modeled.

Bengio et al. replaced discrete counts with a neural network that maps each vocabulary item to a dense, learned vector and predicts the next token from the concatenation of the preceding vectors \citep{bengio2003neural}. This established two ideas that persist through every subsequent architecture discussed in this chapter: representing words as continuous embeddings rather than one-hot symbols, and training the representation and the predictive model jointly. Word2Vec \citep{mikolov2013distributed} and GloVe \citep{pennington2014glove} later factored the embedding step out as a standalone, context-independent pretraining objective, producing reusable word vectors consumed by task-specific models. A limitation shared by all of these is that a word receives exactly one vector regardless of context: ``bank'' is encoded identically in ``river bank'' and ``bank account.'' ELMo addressed this by deriving contextual embeddings from a bidirectional LSTM's hidden states, so a word's representation varies with its sentence \citep{peters2018deep}. Context-dependent representation is a prerequisite for the architecture described next, and reappears in \S\ref{sec:embedding-models} as the basis of the sentence-level embedding models both systems in this thesis use for similarity search.

\subsection{Transformer architecture and autoregressive models}
\label{sec:transformer}

The Transformer replaced recurrence with self-attention: every token's representation is updated by attending to every other token in the sequence in parallel, weighted by learned compatibility scores \citep{vaswani2017attention}. Removing recurrence removes the sequential dependency that made recurrent networks slow to train on long sequences, and self-attention gives every pair of tokens a direct path to one another regardless of distance, which mitigates the degradation in how much distant context recurrent networks retain in practice.

Three families of models built on the Transformer differ in how they use attention. Encoder models such as BERT attend bidirectionally — every token sees the whole input, including tokens to its right — and are trained with a masked-token prediction objective, which suits representation learning for classification and extraction rather than open-ended generation \citep{devlin2019bert}. Encoder-decoder models such as T5 pair a bidirectional encoder over the input with an autoregressive decoder over the output, casting every task as text-to-text \citep{raffel2020exploring}. Decoder-only, autoregressive models such as the GPT family mask attention to the left context only, so each token is predicted from its predecessors alone, and generation proceeds one token at a time by repeatedly sampling from this conditional distribution \citep{radford2018improving,brown2020language}. This thesis uses \emph{LLM} to refer to the decoder-only, autoregressive family, since it is the family the systems in Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} consume through an API; encoder models still appear in both systems, but as embedding models (\S\ref{sec:embedding-models}) rather than as generators.

Transformers do not operate on words but on subword tokens produced by a learned segmentation of the input string. Byte-pair encoding merges frequent character pairs into a fixed-size vocabulary, so common words become single tokens while rare words decompose into several pieces, which bounds vocabulary size without ever failing on an unseen word \citep{sennrich2016neural}; SentencePiece performs the same segmentation directly on raw text without requiring language-specific pre-tokenization, which matters for consistent handling of languages that do not delimit words by whitespace \citep{kudo2018sentencepiece}. Tokenization determines what a model's context window and per-call cost are measured in, and it is the unit in which the token accounting reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} is expressed.

Scale is a defining property of this family. Training loss decreases as a smooth, predictable function of model size, dataset size, and compute, a relationship formalized as scaling laws \citep{kaplan2020scaling} and later refined by Chinchilla's finding that many early models were undertrained relative to their parameter count for the compute budget spent on them \citep{hoffmann2022training}. GPT-3 demonstrated that scale alone — without gradient updates at inference time — produces a model capable of performing new tasks from a handful of examples given in its input, a behavior termed few-shot in-context learning \citep{brown2020language}.

\subsection{Instruction-tuned LLMs and prompting}
\label{sec:instruction-tuning}

A base LLM trained only to predict the next token continues a prompt with what is statistically likely, which is not the same as what is requested. FLAN showed that fine-tuning on a large collection of tasks reformulated as natural-language instructions improves zero-shot performance on unseen tasks \citep{wei2022finetuned}. InstructGPT went further by fine-tuning first on human-written demonstrations and then on human preference comparisons via reinforcement learning, an alignment procedure known as RLHF that builds on preference-based reward learning \citep{christiano2017deep,ouyang2022training}. Instruction-tuned models follow a natural-language instruction directly, which is the interaction mode every LLM call in this thesis relies on: the caller writes a prompt, not a task-specific fine-tuning set.

Alongside proprietary models accessed only through an API, several families of open-weight instruction-tuned models are distributed for direct use — Llama \citep{touvron2023llamab,grattafiori2024llama}, Mistral \citep{jiang2023mistral}, Qwen \citep{bai2023qwen}, and DeepSeek \citep{deepseekai2024deepseek} among them. Both systems in Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} reach models of both kinds through a single OpenAI-compatible gateway rather than binding to one provider, which makes the choice of model a configuration parameter rather than an architectural commitment.

Prompting is the practice of shaping a model's output through the text of the input alone, without updating any weights \citep{liu2023pre}. Zero-shot prompting states the task with no examples; few-shot prompting prepends a small number of input-output demonstrations \citep{brown2020language}. Chain-of-thought prompting elicits intermediate reasoning steps before the final answer, which measurably improves performance on multi-step reasoning tasks, and the improvement persists even with a single generic instruction (``let us think step by step'') rather than task-specific demonstrations \citep{wei2022chain,kojima2022large}. Chain-of-thought underpins two mechanisms used later in this thesis: self-consistency voting (\S\ref{sec:self-consistency}) operates over independently sampled chain-of-thought completions, and the multi-hop retrieval loop of \S\ref{sec:graphrag} scores and prunes reasoning paths step by step rather than answering in one pass.

\subsection{Structured and constrained output from an LLM}
\label{sec:structured-output}

An autoregressive LLM's native output is unstructured text. Both systems in this thesis instead require output conforming to a fixed schema — a specific set of fields with specific types — so that it can be parsed and consumed programmatically without further natural-language interpretation. Two strategies produce such output, and they differ in what they guarantee.

The first constrains decoding itself. At each generation step the set of tokens the model may sample is restricted to those consistent with a target grammar or JSON schema, so the output is well formed by construction rather than by post-hoc validation \citep{willard2023efficient}; subsequent engines reduce the runtime overhead of maintaining this constraint to the point where it is negligible relative to generation itself \citep{dong2024xgrammar}. The guarantee is syntactic: constrained decoding ensures the output parses against the schema, and says nothing about whether the values it contains are correct.

The second strategy exposes the schema to the model as part of the prompt — as a function or tool signature — and relies on instruction-following behavior (\S\ref{sec:instruction-tuning}) to produce a matching payload, validating after the fact and retrying on failure. Instructor layers this pattern on Pydantic: a schema is declared once as a Pydantic model, passed to the LLM call, and the returned payload is parsed and validated against that same model, with a validation error triggering an automatic retry that appends the error to the prompt \citep{liu2026instructor,colvin2026pydantic}. Because validation is ordinary Python, arbitrary semantic constraints can be enforced in the same pass as type checking — a property Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} exploits to reject any extracted field that is populated but lacks a supporting verbatim quote.

Neither strategy is free. Constraining an LLM's output format can degrade the quality of its reasoning relative to the same task posed in free text, so schema design is a performance-relevant decision and not only an interface one \citep{tam2024let}, and reliability degrades measurably as schemas grow deeper and larger \citep{geng2025jsonschemabench}. Restructuring the schema itself — decomposing one large extraction into several smaller ones — is a documented mitigation \citep{shrimal2025parse}, and is the reason Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} partitions extraction into per-topic groups rather than requesting all fields in a single call. A related design point is abstention: a closed vocabulary that offers no way to say \emph{the document does not state this} forces the model to choose a wrong value, so an explicit abstention option is part of the schema rather than an afterthought.

Schema-constrained generation is now the standard interface for information extraction from documents, where the task is to populate a predefined structure from unstructured text \citep{agrawal2022large,sainz2024gollie}, including in the contract and legal domain that Chapter~\ref{ch:systems}'s extraction system operates in \citep{hendrycks2021cuad}.

\subsection{Known limitations of LLMs relevant to this thesis}
\label{sec:llm-limitations}

Three limitations motivate specific design choices in both systems and are referred back to throughout Chapter~\ref{ch:systems}.

\textbf{Hallucination.} An LLM can generate text that is fluent and confident but unsupported by, or in direct conflict with, any grounding source \citep{ji2023survey,huang2023survey}. Detection approaches include comparing multiple sampled generations for mutual consistency \citep{manakul2023selfcheckgpt} and probing whether a model's own confidence correlates with correctness \citep{kadavath2022language,kuhn2023semantic}. The evidence-quote requirement described in Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} — every extracted field carries a verbatim source quote, and a field without one is rejected before it reaches the output — targets this failure mode at generation time rather than detecting it afterwards.

\textbf{Non-determinism and run-to-run variance.} Repeated calls to the same LLM with the same prompt at temperature zero are not guaranteed to return identical output: floating-point non-associativity under varying batching and kernel-scheduling conditions on the serving side can change results between calls even when sampling is deterministic \citep{he2025defeating}. A pipeline expected to be reproducible therefore cannot infer determinism from a fixed temperature setting, which is why Chapter~\ref{ch:results} measures output variance directly instead of assuming it away.

\textbf{The need for external grounding.} An LLM's parametric knowledge is fixed at training time and its generations are not intrinsically verifiable against a source, so tasks requiring up-to-date, document-specific, or auditable facts cannot rely on the model's output alone. Retrieval-augmented generation, treated next, is the dominant architectural response.

\section{Retrieval Augmentation and GraphRAG}
\label{sec:rag-graphrag}

\subsection{Retrieval-augmented generation: motivation, architecture, limits}
\label{sec:rag}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) addresses the grounding limitation of \S\ref{sec:llm-limitations} by conditioning generation on text retrieved from an external corpus at inference time, rather than on parameters learned during training alone \citep{lewis2020retrieval}. The canonical architecture has two stages: a retriever selects a small set of passages relevant to the query, typically by dense vector similarity between a query embedding and a precomputed index of passage embeddings \citep{karpukhin2020dense}, and a generator conditions on the query concatenated with those passages to produce its answer. This decouples factual capacity from parameter count — the corpus, not the weights, is the source of truth — and lets the corpus be updated without retraining \citep{gao2023retrieval}.

Flat RAG has two structural limits that motivate the graph-based extension in \S\ref{sec:graphrag}. First, retrieval treats passages as an unordered bag: each is scored for similarity to the query independently, so nothing in the architecture represents relationships \emph{between} passages, which makes questions requiring evidence combined from several documents hard to answer in a single retrieval pass \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}. Multi-hop methods address this by interleaving retrieval with intermediate reasoning, retrieving again on the basis of what a partial chain of thought has inferred rather than only on the original query \citep{trivedi2023interleaving}. Second, retrieval is local by construction: it surfaces passages resembling the query, not passages relevant to a thematic context the query does not literally mention. Position effects compound both limits, since correctly retrieved evidence is used unevenly by the generator depending on where it falls in the context window \citep{liu2024lost}.

A further line of work makes the pipeline adaptive rather than fixed, letting the system decide when to retrieve and whether retrieved evidence is good enough to use \citep{asai2024self}. Evaluating such systems is itself an open problem: retrieval quality and generation faithfulness must be assessed separately, and reference-free frameworks exist precisely because ground-truth answer sets are rarely available in applied settings \citep{es2024ragas,chen2024benchmarking} — a constraint Chapter~\ref{ch:results} confronts directly.

\subsection{GraphRAG: knowledge graphs, community detection, and multi-hop reasoning}
\label{sec:graphrag}

GraphRAG replaces the flat passage index with a knowledge graph extracted from the corpus by an LLM, and replaces similarity-only retrieval with retrieval guided by graph structure \citep{edge2024local}. Two properties of a knowledge graph answer the two limits of flat RAG directly: entities and relations are extracted once at ingestion time, which makes cross-document connections explicit as edges rather than implicit in embedding proximity, and traversal along those edges gives retrieval a mechanism for multi-hop reasoning that a single similarity query cannot express.

Microsoft's GraphRAG builds this graph automatically — an LLM extracts entities, relations, and claims from source documents — and applies the Leiden algorithm to partition the graph into hierarchical communities of densely connected nodes \citep{edge2024local,traag2019louvain}. Leiden improves on its predecessor Louvain by guaranteeing that every detected community is internally connected, which Louvain does not \citep{traag2019louvain,blondel2008fast}. Each community is then summarized by an LLM, and query answering combines the relevant summaries; this is GraphRAG's global search mode, aimed at questions about a corpus as a whole rather than a specific fact.

Community summarization is not the retrieval pattern this thesis's systems use. What carries forward from GraphRAG is the more general property: a knowledge graph built by an LLM from unstructured text, serving simultaneously as a provenance record (an answer points to the specific edges that produced it) and as a substrate for iterative traversal. Subsequent work explores different retrieval strategies over such a graph — HippoRAG's memory-inspired indexing \citep{gutierrez2024hipporag}, Think-on-Graph's LLM-guided path exploration \citep{sun2024think} — and the design space is now large enough to have been surveyed as a field in its own right \citep{peng2024graph,pan2024unifying}. Sections~\ref{sec:coldrag-paper} and \ref{sec:dial-kg} narrow this family to the two frameworks Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} adapts directly.

\section{The Cold-Start Problem in Recommender Systems}
\label{sec:cold-start}

\subsection{Collaborative filtering, content-based, and hybrid approaches}
\label{sec:cf-content-hybrid}

Collaborative filtering (CF) recommends items from patterns in a user-item interaction matrix alone, on the assumption that users who agreed in the past will agree again. Item-based CF scores a candidate by its co-interaction similarity to items the user has already engaged with \citep{sarwar2001item}; matrix factorization learns low-dimensional latent vectors for users and items whose inner product approximates observed interaction strength \citep{koren2009matrix}. Because most real interaction data records implicit signals (a click, a view) rather than explicit ratings, variants reformulate the objective around confidence-weighted implicit feedback \citep{hu2008collaborative} or around ranking pairs of observed and unobserved items \citep{rendle2009bpr}, and later work replaces the inner product with a learned neural interaction function \citep{he2017neural}. Ranking quality across all these methods is conventionally measured with position-discounted metrics such as NDCG \citep{jarvelin2002cumulated}. CF requires no item or user attributes, only interaction history — simultaneously its strength (domain-agnostic) and its structural weakness: an entity with no interaction history offers nothing to factorize.

Content-based filtering compares item or user attributes directly, recommending items close in feature space to what a user has liked. It needs no interaction history and is therefore immune to that failure, at the cost of requiring informative, well-structured attributes as input — which is precisely what the extraction system of Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} exists to produce. Hybrid systems combine the two, and Burke's survey frames the combination along several axes (weighting, switching, feature combination) rather than as a single fixed design \citep{burke2002hybrid}. Knowledge-graph-based recommenders are a structured variant of this hybridization, propagating user preferences over a graph of item attributes and relations so that side information contributes to ranking alongside collaborative signal \citep{wang2019kgat,guo2022survey}.

\subsection{Item cold-start versus user cold-start}
\label{sec:item-vs-user-coldstart}

Cold-start is the failure of collaborative filtering under absent or sparse interaction history, and it takes two distinct forms. User cold-start affects a new user with no interaction history: there is nothing to factorize on the user side of the matrix. Item cold-start is the symmetric case for a newly listed item. The two differ in remedy as well as in cause. User cold-start is commonly handled by eliciting explicit preferences or profile attributes at onboarding — the approach the conversational module of \S\ref{sec:hitl} takes.

Item cold-start instead requires substituting content signal for interaction signal until history accumulates, and several strategies do so. DropoutNet trains a model to reconstruct latent factors from content features alone, simulating the cold condition during training \citep{volkovs2017dropoutnet}. MeLU applies meta-learning so the model adapts to a new item or user from very few interactions \citep{lee2019melu}. Heater and CLCRec align content-based and collaborative embedding spaces so that a content-only representation is directly usable for ranking \citep{zhu2020recommendation,wei2021contrastive}, and ALDI distills the behavior of a warm-item model into a cold-item one \citep{huang2023aligning}. What these share is a training stage over an existing interaction corpus — a requirement that is not always satisfiable, and is not satisfied in the deployment setting of this thesis.

The matching system described in Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} addresses item cold-start under exactly that constraint: a newly ingested asset has no interaction history by construction, no historical interaction corpus is available to train an alignment model on, and matching must therefore proceed from extracted content alone.

\subsection{LLM-based zero-shot recommenders}
\label{sec:llm-zeroshot-rec}

A separate line of work replaces learned collaborative or content embeddings with an LLM's parametric and in-context reasoning, framing recommendation as a language task. P5 unifies several recommendation subtasks — rating prediction, sequential recommendation, explanation — under one prompted text-to-text model \citep{geng2022recommendation}; TALLRec fine-tunes an LLM on a small set of recommendation-specific instructions to align its general language ability with the task \citep{bao2023tallrec}; RecMind wraps an LLM as an agent that plans and calls external tools rather than answering from a single prompt \citep{wang2024recmind}. Without any task-specific training at all, prompted LLMs exhibit non-trivial zero-shot ranking ability \citep{dai2023uncovering,hou2024large}, and LLMRec augments that capacity with a graph structure over items \citep{wei2024llmrec}. Recent surveys identify cold-start as the setting where this family is most attractive, precisely because it requires no interaction-derived embedding for the cold item to exist in the first place \citep{zhang2025cold,wu2024survey,lin2025how}.

\subsection{The ColdRAG paper and its relation to this thesis's work}
\label{sec:coldrag-paper}

Yang et al. propose ColdRAG, a retrieval-augmented framework for cold-start recommendation that builds a knowledge graph dynamically from item content and retrieves candidates through LLM-guided multi-hop reasoning over that graph, rather than through an embedding learned from interaction history \citep{yang2025adaptive}. It combines the item-cold-start remedy of \S\ref{sec:item-vs-user-coldstart} — content substituting for absent interactions — with the graph-structured retrieval of \S\ref{sec:graphrag}, and requires no training stage over historical interactions, which is what makes it applicable in the setting described above.

The system presented in this thesis carries the same name and is a domain-specific implementation adapted from that paper for real-estate off-market intermediation at Dometria AI. It is not the authors' original codebase, which is maintained separately. Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} states at each point of divergence where the Dometria implementation departs from the published design; the largest departure is the ingestion pipeline, which follows DIAL-KG (\S\ref{sec:dial-kg}) rather than the graph-construction method described in the ColdRAG paper.

\section{Knowledge Graph Construction}
\label{sec:kg-construction}

\subsection{Rule-based, supervised, and LLM-based approaches}
\label{sec:kgc-approaches}

Knowledge graph construction extracts structured entities and relations from unstructured text. Open information extraction systems such as TextRunner and ReVerb extract subject-relation-object triples using hand-built or lightly supervised syntactic patterns, with no fixed target schema \citep{banko2007open,fader2011identifying}. Distant supervision instead trains a relation classifier by heuristically aligning an existing knowledge base with text — a sentence is treated as expressing a relation whenever the entity pair it mentions is already so related in the knowledge base — trading manual annotation for dependence on a fixed external schema \citep{mintz2009distant}.

LLM-based construction replaces both the hand-built patterns and the schema-aligned classifier with a prompted LLM that extracts entities and relations directly, using pretrained language understanding in place of a task-specific supervised model \citep{zhu2024llms,zhang2024extract}. This moves the bottleneck from labeled training data to prompt design and output validation, and it imports the reliability concerns of \S\ref{sec:llm-limitations} — hallucinated entities and relations, variance across ingestion runs — into the graph-building step itself. Because a knowledge graph is persistent, an error admitted at ingestion contaminates every later query that traverses it, which is why the validation mechanisms of \S\ref{sec:llm-as-judge} are placed downstream of extraction as a gate rather than treated as optional quality tooling.

\subsection{Schema-free and incremental construction}
\label{sec:schema-free-kgc}

A schema fixed before construction begins is a strong assumption when the corpus is heterogeneous or evolving: a schema adequate for the first batch of documents may not accommodate entity types or relations that only appear later. Schema-free construction lets the schema emerge from the data, inferring entity and relation types as documents are processed instead of validating against a predefined ontology \citep{carta2023iterative}. Incremental construction processes documents one at a time or in small batches, merging each extraction into a graph that already exists rather than building the graph once from a static corpus \citep{lairgi2024itext2kg}.

The two properties compose naturally, and they compose into a requirement: an incremental pipeline must decide, for every newly extracted entity, whether it is the same as one already in the graph or genuinely new — without a fixed schema to lean on for that decision. Getting it wrong in one direction fragments a single real-world entity across duplicate nodes; getting it wrong in the other collapses two distinct entities and creates edges that were never supported by any document. This is the disambiguation problem taken up in \S\ref{sec:ned}.

\subsection{The DIAL-KG framework}
\label{sec:dial-kg}

DIAL-KG, proposed by Bao et al., is a schema-free, incremental knowledge graph construction framework built around three components that Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} refers to by name \citep{bao2026dial}. A Meta-Knowledge Base (MKB) accumulates entity profiles across ingestion runs and serves as the persistent context against which each new document's extraction is disambiguated and merged, so documents are not processed in isolation. Dual-track extraction separates the extraction of graph-worthy entities and relations from the extraction of document- or product-level identifying information, letting the two tracks be validated and merged independently. An incremental adaptation cycle governs how graph and MKB evolve together: each ingestion contributes new nodes and edges and updates the entity profiles that the next ingestion will be checked against.

Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} implements its ingestion pipeline against this framework, and the MKB, dual-track extraction, and incremental cycle described here are the direct source of the correspondingly named components there. DIAL-KG is a construction framework this thesis's system implements against; it is not part of the ColdRAG paper's published inference method (\S\ref{sec:coldrag-paper}), and the two are combined in this work rather than in either source.

\subsection{Named entity disambiguation, coreference resolution, entity linking}
\label{sec:ned}

Three related problems arise once extracted entities must be merged into a shared graph rather than kept per-document. Named entity disambiguation (NED) decides whether two entity mentions refer to the same real-world entity — the decision \S\ref{sec:schema-free-kgc} identified as unavoidable in any incremental pipeline. Coreference resolution is the narrower, intra-document version: deciding which mentions within a single text, including pronouns and other referring expressions, denote the same entity \citep{lee2017end}. Entity linking poses the problem against an external inventory of already-identified entities, asking which catalog entry a mention corresponds to, and is commonly solved by dense retrieval over entity representations followed by re-ranking \citep{wu2020scalable,sevgili2022neural}.

All three have moved from dedicated supervised models toward LLM prompting, and LLMs are competitive at entity matching when the decision hinges on the text at hand \citep{peeters2025entity}. They are not uniformly reliable as coreference resolvers, particularly on cases requiring world knowledge outside the immediate text \citep{le2023large}. This asymmetry motivates a two-stage design rather than a single LLM call: vector similarity over entity embeddings proposes a small candidate set, and an LLM judge adjudicates only within it. Retrieval bounds the comparison to plausible candidates and the judge supplies the semantic decision that similarity alone cannot make. Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} describes this two-stage arrangement as the NED mechanism of the ingestion pipeline.

\section{Self-Consistency and Validation via LLMs}
\label{sec:self-consistency-validation}

\subsection{Self-consistency and majority voting}
\label{sec:self-consistency}

Chain-of-thought prompting (\S\ref{sec:instruction-tuning}) produces one reasoning path per generation, and that path can be wrong even when the model is, in aggregate, capable of reasoning correctly about the problem. Self-consistency samples several independent chain-of-thought completions for the same prompt at non-zero temperature and takes a majority vote over their final answers, discarding the reasoning paths and keeping only what they converge to \citep{wang2023self}. Errors in reasoning are not perfectly correlated across independent samples, whereas a correct answer is a comparatively stable attractor that distinct paths tend to reach, so the vote is more reliable than any single sample.

Wang et al. report consistent gains over chain-of-thought prompting alone across reasoning benchmarks: GSM8K improves by 17.9 percentage points, SVAMP by 11.0, AQuA by 12.2, StrategyQA by 6.4, and ARC-challenge by 3.9 \citep{wang2023self}. The cost is linear in the number of samples, which makes it a mechanism to apply selectively rather than everywhere; Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} restricts voting to mandatory and high-priority field groups for exactly this reason. Related mechanisms reuse the same insight differently, having a model revise its own output against self-generated feedback \citep{madaan2023self} or verify a candidate answer by reasoning backwards from it \citep{weng2023large}.

\subsection{LLM-as-judge and entailment gating}
\label{sec:llm-as-judge}

A second use of an LLM is not to produce an answer but to evaluate one already produced, whether by itself, another model, or a preceding pipeline stage. LLM-as-judge substitutes model judgment for human evaluation or a fixed metric, prompting the model to score or compare candidates against stated criteria \citep{zheng2023judging}. A judge is subject to the limitations of \S\ref{sec:llm-limitations} like any other LLM call, and characterizing judge biases and their agreement with human raters is an active research area rather than a settled question \citep{gu2024survey}.

Entailment gating narrows the judge pattern to grounding specifically. Instead of scoring quality along an open rubric, the judge answers a binary question — is this claimed value entailed by this specific cited span of source text? — and the claim is accepted only on a positive answer. The narrowing is what makes the pattern usable as a gate: a binary, evidence-bounded question is far more tractable than an open-ended quality judgment, and it fails safe, since rejecting a well-supported claim costs recall while admitting an unsupported one corrupts the store. This verification is applied after generation and is complementary to constrained decoding (\S\ref{sec:structured-output}), which guarantees that output parses but says nothing about whether it is supported. Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} describes two instances: the governance layer's evidence check during graph construction, and the retrieval engine's LLM edge scoring, which gates which edges a reasoning path may continue through.

\section{Vector Search and Embeddings}
\label{sec:vector-search}

\subsection{Embedding models and cosine similarity}
\label{sec:embedding-models}

A text embedding model maps a span of text to a fixed-length dense vector such that semantic similarity between spans corresponds to geometric proximity between vectors. Proximity is most commonly measured by cosine similarity, the cosine of the angle between two vectors, which is invariant to magnitude and therefore isolates direction — semantic content — from length effects. Modern embedding models are trained contrastively: semantically related pairs are pulled together in embedding space and unrelated pairs pushed apart, a paradigm established at sentence level by Sentence-BERT \citep{reimers2019sentence}.

Multilinguality does not follow automatically, since nothing in a monolingual objective places a sentence and its translation nearby. Distilling a monolingual teacher into a multilingual student is one solution \citep{reimers2020making,conneau2020unsupervised}; BGE-M3 instead trains multilinguality, multiple granularities from sentence to long passage, and multiple retrieval functions — dense, sparse, and multi-vector — jointly in a single model \citep{chen2024m3}. Comparison across models is standardized by the MTEB benchmark suite, which evaluates embeddings over a broad range of retrieval and similarity tasks rather than a single dataset \citep{muennighoff2023mteb}.

Both systems in Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} operate on Italian source documents, which makes multilingual capability a requirement rather than a preference, and both use models from the families introduced here: a distilled multilingual MiniLM for contract-category matching, and BGE-M3 for the knowledge graph's entity embeddings.

\subsection{Vector indices and nearest-neighbor search}
\label{sec:ann-indices}

Exact nearest-neighbor search compares a query vector against every stored vector, scaling linearly with index size and becoming the retrieval bottleneck as a corpus grows. Approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) methods trade a small, controllable amount of recall for large speedups. Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs (HNSW) build a multi-layer proximity graph over the indexed vectors and search it greedily from a coarse top layer down to a fine bottom layer, achieving logarithmic-time search in practice at high recall \citep{malkov2020efficient}; other index families quantize vectors to trade memory for accuracy, and mature libraries implement several such strategies behind one interface \citep{johnson2021billion}. Dense retrieval is also frequently combined with lexical scoring such as BM25, since the two fail on different queries — exact identifiers and rare terms favor lexical matching, paraphrase favors dense \citep{robertson2009probabilistic}.

Where a system already stores a knowledge graph, keeping the vector index inside the same database avoids maintaining consistency between two stores that must be traversed together. Chapter~\ref{ch:systems}'s retrieval engine takes this approach, issuing vector search and graph traversal against a single Neo4j instance with a native vector index rather than pairing a standalone ANN library with a separate graph store \citep{neo4j2026neo4j}.

\section{Human-in-the-Loop Data Acquisition}
\label{sec:hitl}

\subsection{Structured validation, approval gates, and confidence-based routing}
\label{sec:hitl-validation}

Human-in-the-loop (HITL) design keeps a person in the decision path where full automation is unreliable or unaccountable, rather than automating end to end \citep{amershi2014power}. Mixed-initiative interaction frames this as shared control: system and user each act where they are more capable, and the interface must make clear at every turn which party is expected to act \citep{horvitz1999principles}. Active learning is a related but distinct pattern in which the system selects \emph{which} items to route to a human — the most informative or most uncertain — rather than routing all or none \citep{settles2009active}. Confidence-based routing applies that selection principle per field or per turn, deciding whether a value is confident enough to accept or must be confirmed.

In conversational settings this takes the form of a clarifying question issued when required information is missing or ambiguous, rather than a guess \citep{aliannejadi2019asking,zamani2020generating}. The distinction matters for a system whose downstream output is a structured record: an inferred value is indistinguishable from a stated one once written, so asking is the only way to keep the record's provenance intact. Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} describes a conversational module built on this principle, which asks a targeted follow-up for each missing mandatory field and proceeds only once all of them validate.

\subsection{LlamaIndex Workflows: event-driven orchestration}
\label{sec:llamaindex-workflows}

LlamaIndex Workflows structures a multi-step process as typed steps connected by typed events: each step declares the event type it consumes and the type it emits, and the engine dispatches control to whichever step matches the event just produced, instead of following a hand-coded control-flow graph \citep{liu2026llamaindex}. Events are Pydantic models (\S\ref{sec:structured-output}), so data crossing a step boundary is schema-validated at the hand-off. A persistent Context object accompanies a run and lets steps read and write shared state.

Two properties make this a fit for conversational data acquisition. A multi-turn process must accumulate partial answers across turns before all mandatory fields are filled, which the Context provides; and the control flow is genuinely data-dependent — which question comes next depends on what is still missing — which event dispatch expresses more directly than a fixed sequence of calls. Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} describes the conversational module as an instance of this pattern, with session state persisted externally between turns so a conversation survives beyond the lifetime of a single process.

\section{Differential Privacy}
\label{sec:differential-privacy}

\subsection{$\varepsilon$-differential privacy, the Laplace mechanism, sensitivity, privacy budget}
\label{sec:dp-definition}

Differential privacy formalizes a guarantee about a data-releasing mechanism rather than about a dataset. A randomized mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $\varepsilon$-differential privacy if, for every pair of datasets $D$ and $D'$ differing in a single record and every measurable set $S$ of possible outputs,
\begin{equation}
\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \;\leq\; e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S],
\label{eq:dp}
\end{equation}
so no observed output makes it possible to infer confidently whether any particular record was included in the input \citep{dwork2006differential,dwork2014algorithmic}. The parameter $\varepsilon$, the privacy budget, controls the trade-off directly: a smaller $\varepsilon$ bounds the ratio in Equation~\ref{eq:dp} more tightly and gives a stronger guarantee at the cost of more distortion in genuine results; a larger $\varepsilon$ does the reverse.

The Laplace mechanism achieves this guarantee for a numeric query $f$ by adding noise drawn from a Laplace distribution centered at zero with scale $\Delta f / \varepsilon$, where the sensitivity
\begin{equation}
\Delta f \;=\; \max_{D, D'} \lVert f(D) - f(D') \rVert_1
\label{eq:sensitivity}
\end{equation}
is the largest change a single record can induce in the query's true output \citep{dwork2006calibrating}. Sensitivity, not $\varepsilon$ alone, determines how much a specific output is perturbed: a query that no individual record can move far tolerates proportionally less noise for the same guarantee. The guarantee also degrades under repetition — answering many queries over the same data consumes budget cumulatively — which is why $\varepsilon$ is a system-level parameter rather than a per-query one.

Applying differential privacy to recommendation specifically is well established, since released recommendations are themselves a function of the underlying records and can leak information about them \citep{mcsherry2009differentially}. Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} applies the Laplace mechanism to numeric metadata before candidate results are exposed to a user, with the privacy budget exposed as a configuration parameter; \S\ref{sec:blind-matching} explains why that exposure is a domain requirement and not merely a precaution.

\section{Domain Context: Real-Estate Valuation and the Off-Market Sector}
\label{sec:domain-context}

\subsection{Valuation standards: RICS, IVS, EVS}
\label{sec:valuation-standards}

Real-estate valuation in Europe is governed by overlapping professional standards rather than a single statute. The RICS Red Book Global Standards set out valuation bases, mandatory reporting content, and conduct requirements for members of the Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors \citep{royalinstitutionofcharteredsurveyors2024rics}. The International Valuation Standards, maintained by the IVSC, define bases and methodology at a jurisdiction-agnostic level intended for adoption or reference by national standards \citep{internationalvaluationstandardscouncil2024international}. The European Valuation Standards, maintained by TEGoVA, occupy an intermediate position and harmonize practice across European jurisdictions \citep{tegova2025european}. For Italy, Tecnoborsa's \emph{Codice delle Valutazioni Immobiliari} supplies the national standard whose field and category conventions an extraction pipeline over Italian real-estate documents must be consistent with \citep{tecnoborsa2025codice}.

These standards are cited for terminology and for the attribute conventions the extraction schema of Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} aligns to. Neither system performs standard-compliant valuation, and no claim of compliance is made. The theoretical link between attributes and value is nevertheless the one that makes attribute extraction worth doing at all: hedonic pricing treats a property as a bundle of characteristics whose implicit prices are recoverable from market data, so a structured, comparable attribute record is the input any downstream valuation or comparison depends on \citep{rosen1974hedonic}.

\subsection{Blind matching and confidential deal-flow}
\label{sec:blind-matching}

Off-market intermediation — matching an asset with a counterparty before or without a public listing — operates under confidentiality constraints that public-market matching does not. A seller's identity, an asset's precise location, or the fact that it is for sale at all is frequently sensitive, disclosed to a prospective counterparty only as a deal progresses, typically gated by a non-disclosure agreement and, once due diligence begins, exchanged through a virtual data room rather than openly. Blind matching is the corresponding practice on the discovery side: a prospective match is presented using non-identifying summary attributes only, with full disclosure deferred until both parties have expressed interest.

This is the practical motivation for the anonymization step of \S\ref{sec:dp-definition}. A system that streams candidate metadata to a user during exploration exposes numeric attributes repeatedly and across many queries, and attributes precise enough to be useful for matching are also precise enough to re-identify a listing when combined — which is exactly the accumulation-over-queries risk that a formal privacy budget bounds and an ad-hoc rounding rule does not.

Two classical results frame the problem the matching system addresses. Information asymmetry over hidden quality degrades a market when the informed party cannot credibly disclose and the uninformed party cannot verify \citep{akerlof1970market}, which is the condition confidential deal-flow creates deliberately and which staged disclosure is designed to manage. And the underlying task — pairing two sides of a market according to mutual preference — is the subject of two-sided matching theory \citep{gale1962college,roth1990two}, whose framing of a match as jointly acceptable to both parties, rather than as a ranked list produced for one, is the sense in which Chapter~\ref{ch:systems} uses the term.
